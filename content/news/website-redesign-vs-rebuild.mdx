Most websites are replaced for the wrong reason: someone senior got bored of it. The better question is whether the site still does its job, and if not, whether the problem sits in the surface or the foundations.

## When should you redesign your website?

You should redesign your website when it no longer reflects what the business sells, who it sells to, or how you want to be perceived, and when the data shows visitors are not converting as they should. Age alone is not a reason. A five-year-old site that loads in under two seconds and books meetings is doing better than a two-year-old one that does neither.

The strongest triggers are strategic rather than aesthetic. A new proposition, a new market, a merger, or a shift from selling services to selling a product all change what the homepage must say and what the navigation must prioritise. If you have just been through a [rebrand or brand refresh](/news/rebrand-vs-refresh), the website is usually where the gap between old and new first becomes visible to customers.

Quieter signals build up over time:

- Your team says "don't look at the website" before a pitch or an investor meeting.
- Mobile visitors, now 55 to 65 per cent of traffic for most B2B sites, convert at a fraction of desktop.

Three to five years is a reasonable cycle for a meaningful redesign, but the calendar should confirm the decision, not make it.

## What is the difference between a redesign and a rebuild?

A redesign changes what visitors see and read: layout, visual language, copy, page structure and user journeys, while keeping the underlying platform, hosting and codebase largely intact. A rebuild replaces those foundations: a new content management system, a new front-end architecture, new integrations and often a new information architecture from the ground up. One is a renovation; the other is a new building on the same plot.

The costs, risks and timelines differ sharply. A redesign on a sound platform might take eight to twelve weeks with little technical risk. A rebuild typically runs three to six months and involves content migration, URL redirects, re-implementing forms and tracking, and retraining whoever edits the site.

You are in rebuild territory when:

- The CMS is end-of-life, heavily customised, or so locked down that every change needs a developer. It is the same lock-in question you would ask about [custom CRM versus off-the-shelf software](/news/custom-crm-vs-off-the-shelf): the platform has stopped serving you and started dictating to you.
- Core Web Vitals are failing for architectural reasons (bloated themes, dozens of plugins, render-blocking scripts) rather than ones optimisation can fix.
- The site cannot integrate with the tools the business now runs on: CRM, booking, payments, marketing automation.
- Security patches have lapsed because upgrading would break things.

You are in redesign territory when the platform is modern and maintained, page speed is respectable, and the problems are about message, hierarchy, credibility and conversion. An honest audit at the start saves real money: companies often budget for a rebuild and find that a redesign plus a performance clean-up gets 90 per cent of the result.

## What are the signs your website is underperforming?

The clearest signs are measurable: conversion rates below your sector's norm, high bounce on landing pages that should hold attention, rising cost per lead from paid traffic, and organic rankings slipping despite regular content. If the site gets traffic but the pipeline does not move, the site is the bottleneck.

Useful benchmarks:

- **Conversion rate.** B2B service sites typically convert visitors to enquiries at 2 to 4 per cent. Below 1 per cent on relevant traffic points to unclear messaging, weak calls to action or friction in forms.
- **Page speed.** Largest Contentful Paint above 2.5 seconds on mobile is a failing grade in Google's eyes, and every extra second measurably reduces conversion.
- **Mobile performance.** If mobile conversion is under half of desktop, the layout is failing where most of your audience is.
- **Search visibility.** Falling impressions on core terms, or ranking pages with a click-through rate under 2 per cent, suggest titles and content that no longer match intent.
- **Behaviour.** Rage clicks in session recordings, visitors scrolling past the hero, or pricing-page visitors leaving within ten seconds.

Ask five recent customers what the site told them about you before they got in touch; if that does not match your positioning, the analytics will never show it.

## What should a website redesign actually deliver?

A redesign should deliver a measurable improvement in the numbers that matter: more qualified enquiries, better conversion from paid and organic traffic, faster pages, and a site the team can extend without calling an agency every time. If the brief starts and ends with "make it look more modern", the outcome will be a prettier version of the same problems.

A properly scoped redesign starts with the commercial question: who needs to do what on this site, and what stops them today. The deliverables should include:

- **A revised information architecture** that reflects how buyers search and decide, not how the org chart is arranged.
- **Rewritten core pages.** Copy is where most redesigns fail. Homepage, service pages and case studies need proof, specifics and a clear next step.
- **Performance targets in the contract.** Lighthouse above 90 on mobile, LCP under 2.5 seconds, page weight under 1.5 MB. Numbers, not adjectives.
- **Conversion design.** Forms trimmed to the fields you need, calls to action where intent peaks, trust signals near decision points.
- **Analytics set up properly** so you can prove the change worked. A surprising number of redesigns launch with conversion tracking broken.
- **A content and SEO migration plan** with redirects mapped for every URL that earns traffic. Skipping this is how a redesign loses 30 per cent of organic traffic overnight.
- **Editor training**, so the site does not drift back into inconsistency within six months.

## How much does a website redesign cost?

In the UK, a professional redesign for a small or mid-sized business typically costs between £8,000 and £40,000, depending on the number of page templates, the volume of content to rewrite or migrate, and the integrations involved. Full rebuilds with custom functionality and multi-language content regularly reach £50,000 to £150,000 and beyond.

Page count is a poor predictor of cost. What actually drives the figure:

- **Strategy and content.** Research, messaging and copywriting for 15 to 20 core pages can be a third of the budget, and it is the third that moves conversion.
- **Templates, not pages.** A 200-page site on eight good templates costs less than a 30-page site where every page is bespoke.
- **Migration.** Moving 400 blog posts with images, metadata and redirects is real work, and corners cut here show up in search results.
- **Platform.** Switching CMS adds licensing, training and rebuild time.

Judge the spend against what the site should produce. If a redesign lifts enquiry conversion from 1.2 to 2.5 per cent on 8,000 monthly visitors, that is roughly 100 additional enquiries a month, and most redesigns pay for themselves within a year on that basis.

## The takeaway

Decide between redesign and rebuild on evidence, not on how tired the site feels. If the platform is sound, fix the message, the journeys and the speed; if the platform is the problem, replace it and treat the redesign as part of the rebuild. Either way, write the success metrics into the brief before design starts: a website that looks better but converts the same has simply cost you money.
